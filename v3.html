The meeting

It's the quarterly review. Someone pulls up the dashboard. Traffic is up, leads are flat, and pipeline is the number nobody wants to say out loud.

So the room does what rooms do. It debates channels. Someone suggests more content. Someone else wants to try events again. Budget moves from one column to another, and everyone leaves with something to do.

Nobody says the quiet part: five people in that room would describe the ideal customer five different ways, and not one of them has spoken to a buyer this quarter.

The argument was never about channels. It was about a decision nobody had made.

What indecision costs

Serving three segments doesn't divide your results by three.

Winning a deal needs four things true at once: your message fits how they talk, you have proof from a lookalike company, their objection is pre-answered, and you're credible live. Those four multiply — they don't add.

Focused on one segment 41% Four factors at 0.8 each
Split across three 6% The same four at 0.5 each

Three segments at 6% each is 18% of a market's worth of effort, against 41% from one. You didn't lose a third — you lost most of it. Figures are illustrative; the multiplicative shape is the point.

The turn

Better questions first. Everything else is downstream.

"How do we get more leads?" has no answer. "Who is choosing us today, and why?" has one — and once you have it, the channel argument settles itself, because you finally know who you're talking to and what they're comparing you against.

The method

Understand. Define. Build. Learn.

Every engagement moves through the same four stages. The order matters — skip one and the ones after it get weaker.

Stage 1

Understand

Calls, reviews, lost deals, competitor messaging, search and AI answers.

You get: what buyers actually say, and why they choose you.

Stage 2

Define

You get: one problem statement everyone agrees with.

Build

You get: a plan your team can start on Monday.

Stage 4

Learn

Measure what moved, cut what didn't, refine the next decision with better data.

You get: a review that ends in one clear next step.

Case study / 001

The company that was winning deals and losing the market

A 34% win rate looked like proof the product was strong. It was the symptom: they were winning the deals they got shortlisted for, and almost never getting shortlisted. A narrow funnel was flattering the average.

They described themselves as a "people operations platform". Every buyer interviewed said "payroll software". Repositioning into the category buyers actually searched for would roughly double qualified opportunities at flat spend — modelled, holding win rate and budget constant.

The lesson: a high win rate is one of the most dangerous numbers in B2B marketing. Always read it against the volume of opportunities that produced it.

Case study / 002

Invisible to the assistant, and nobody noticed for two quarters

Rankings were stable, traffic was stable, demo requests were quietly falling. Sales began hearing "we had a shortlist already" — naming two competitors the team considered weaker.

Twenty buyer-phrased prompts across three assistants showed why. The competitors didn't rank better; they were simply described more often in the sources assistants lean on — comparison pages, review summaries, forum threads.

The lesson: you can hold every search ranking you ever had and still vanish from the shortlist. Anyone promising a guaranteed position in an AI answer is selling something that cannot be bought.

Case study / 003

The demo that wowed, and the deal that died in procurement

Every first call went well. Prospects called it "magic". Then the second call never got booked — or it got booked with someone asking where the data goes, what happens when it's wrong, and who signs off on it.

The company read this as a sales problem and hired two more AEs. But the enthusiasm and the silence came from two different people: the demo converted an individual, while the purchase required a committee assessing risk.

The lesson: for AI products, capability wins the first meeting. Accountability wins the contract.

FAQ

A few things, answered

What does an engagement cost?
Fixed price per engagement, agreed before anything starts. After the first call you get a single page: the problem as we understand it, the scope, the deliverables, your time commitment and the price. Indian companies are invoiced in INR plus GST; companies elsewhere in USD.
How much of my team's time will this take?
We tell you upfront and then stick to it. A positioning engagement is typically two working sessions and six to eight hours of your team's time in total. If the scope needs to change midway, we re-agree it in writing before continuing.
Why are there no named client case studies yet?
Because Traxn is new, and we won't publish a client's name, numbers or words until they have approved them in writing. The analyses here are labelled illustrative for the same reason. You can judge the thinking without us borrowing someone else's results.
Do you do the execution too?
We do the strategic work and the first layer of execution that proves it. If you need a full execution agency for ads, design and video, or a brand identity built from scratch, we'll say so on the first call and point you to people who do that well.
What if we're not a good fit?
We'll tell you, early and plainly. If you don't need us, or need something we don't do, that's a thirty-minute call rather than a wasted quarter. We'd rather lose a bad-fit project on the first call than three weeks into it.
