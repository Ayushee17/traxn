GTM and market strategy

The five things that change when an Indian SaaS company sells to the US

The product is identical. The deck is translated into nobody's language, because it was already in English. And the pitch stops working. Here is what actually moved.

Ayushi Gupta22 October 20269 min read

The US is usually the first market an Indian B2B SaaS company expands into, and it is usually the one that surprises them most. Not because it's hostile — it's the most accessible large software market in the world — but because it looks familiar enough that teams skip the work they would obviously do before entering Japan or Germany.

English is shared. The buyer has the same job title. The software category exists. So the team takes the deck that has been winning in Bengaluru, changes ₹ to $, and runs it.

What follows is traffic without pipeline, and a slow, expensive argument about whether the problem is the channel, the pricing or the SDR team. It is usually none of those. Five things changed underneath the pitch, and each one invalidates part of a message built at home.

Shift 01

The alternative in the buyer's head

This is the big one, and everything else follows from it. Positioning is always relative to what the buyer would otherwise do — and that reference point moves across the ocean.

Often in India

You are replacing a manual process: spreadsheets, email threads, a person who "handles it". You are the first tool in the category they've bought.

Often in the US

They already own a tool in your category, or have evaluated three. You are a replacement, which is a different and harder sale.

A message built against chaos — "stop losing track of X" — assumes nothing is in place. Said to someone who bought a competitor two years ago, it reads as though you haven't understood their situation. Worse, it signals you're early, and early is a risk.

Ask before the first campaign: for our last ten US conversations, what did they say they do today? If the answer isn't specific, you don't yet know what you're replacing.

Shift 02

What counts as proof

Proof is local. A buyer assesses risk by looking for people like them who survived the decision — and "like them" is geographic far more than teams expect.

Travels poorly

Indian logos unknown to a US buyer. Founder-led demos as the primary trust mechanism. Testimonials without a recognisable company behind them.

Travels well

G2 and Capterra presence. SOC 2. Named US or EU customers, even small ones. A support SLA in their timezone, stated plainly.

The hardest version of this: a company with impressive domestic scale finds that none of it transfers, and has to sell as though it were pre-revenue — while internally feeling like a market leader. That gap between how established you feel and how established you appear is where most US expansions go wrong emotionally, and it leads to pricing and messaging decisions made from frustration.

Ask before the first campaign: if a US buyer tries to check us out without talking to us, what do they find in ten minutes? Go and look. That's your proof surface.

Shift 03

The pricing anchor

Price is read against a reference, not in isolation, and the reference changes completely.

Anchored against

In India, often the cost of the manual process — a junior salary, or the cost of a mistake.

Anchored against

In the US, the incumbent's per-seat fee, which may be several times higher.

This produces the most common and most damaging error of US entry: pricing low because it feels high relative to home. A price far below the category norm doesn't read as value. It reads as missing something — fewer features, weaker security, a company that might not be around in three years. You will spend the entire sales cycle defending the discount you volunteered.

The discipline is to price against the US alternative, and let affordability be a consequence of that comparison rather than the headline of your pitch.

Ask before the first campaign: what does the incumbent charge per seat, and is our price a credible fraction of it — or an unbelievable one?

Shift 04

Who is actually in the room

Deal sizes that one person can approve at home often cross an approval threshold in the US, and the moment they do, new people appear who were never in your pitch.

Security review. Procurement. Legal, on data processing and where it's stored. Sometimes a vendor-consolidation policy that has nothing to do with your product's merits and everything to do with a CFO reducing the number of invoices.

These people never see your demo. They read documents. So a deal can be won on the call and lost in a PDF you haven't written — and the feedback loop is silent, because nobody tells you that procurement killed it. The champion simply stops replying, and your CRM records "went quiet".

Ask before the first campaign: who signs, who reviews, and what document does each of them need? If you can't name them, your pipeline forecast is fiction.

Shift 05

Tone, and the line between confident and overclaiming

The most subtle shift, and the one that's hardest to hear about your own copy.

US B2B marketing tolerates and expects direct, specific, benefit-forward claims — but it punishes unsupported superlatives harder than many teams expect. "World's best", "revolutionary", "#1 platform" without a citation reads as noise at best and as a credibility problem at worst. Meanwhile, formality that signals respect in one market can read as distance in another; a US buyer generally wants the point in the first sentence, not the third paragraph.

The fix isn't to become brash. It's to swap adjectives for specifics. "Leading" is weaker than "used by 40 contract teams in the UK". The sentence is less flattering and considerably more persuasive, and it has the advantage of being checkable — which matters more every year, as buyers increasingly verify claims by asking an AI assistant rather than by reading your site.

Ask before the first campaign: delete every adjective on the homepage. Does anything factual remain?

The product crossed the ocean unchanged. The reference point the buyer judges it against did not — and positioning is nothing but that reference point.

The cheapest version of this work

None of this requires a market-entry consultancy, an office in Austin or a research budget. The minimum viable version is roughly a week of deliberate effort:

  1. Ten conversations with US buyers in your category — not pitches, questions. What do you use today? What nearly stopped you buying it? Who else had to approve it?
  2. Read twenty reviews of your closest US competitor on G2. Reviews are the cheapest buyer research that exists, and people are remarkably candid about what made them switch.
  3. Rewrite one page — not the whole site. The homepage, positioned against the US alternative rather than the Indian one.
  4. Run the five questions above and write the answers down. Where you can't answer, that's the gap, and it's better found now than after a quarter of spend.
A note on what's in this piece. The patterns described here are drawn from go-to-market work with B2B SaaS and AI companies selling between India and the US, UK and Gulf markets. The examples are composites rather than accounts of particular clients, and the contrasts are tendencies, not rules — your category may run entirely against them. Test them against your own ten conversations before trusting them.

The one-sentence version

If you take one thing from this: find out what your US buyer does today without you, before you write anything. Every one of the five shifts is downstream of that answer. Get it right and the rest of the work is editing. Guess at it and you will spend a year optimising a message aimed at a buyer who doesn't exist.
