Positioning and differentiation

Why "we serve three segments" usually means you serve none

Splitting a small team's attention across three segments doesn't divide your results by three. It divides them by considerably more, and the reason is arithmetic rather than effort.

Ayushi Gupta22 October 20267 min read

Ask a Series A founder who they sell to and you will often get three answers in one breath. "Mid-market fintech, some healthcare, and we're seeing real pull from logistics." Each one is true. Each has a logo behind it. And the instinct to keep all three is entirely rational: revenue is revenue, and turning away a willing buyer feels like a luxury a small company can't afford.

The problem is that serving three segments is not three times the work. It's closer to nine, and the cost shows up in a place nobody is measuring.

The arithmetic nobody runs

Here is the thing that makes focus hard to argue about in the abstract and easy to argue about with numbers. Winning a B2B deal in a given segment requires a stack of things to be true at once:

  • Your message names the problem the way that segment names it
  • You have proof from a company that looks like them
  • You've answered the objection their function always raises
  • Someone on your team knows the domain well enough to be credible in a live conversation

Each of those is a probability, not a binary. A focused team might run at 0.8 on each — not perfect, but consistently good. Multiply them and you get a win rate of roughly 41%.

Now split the same team across three segments. Nobody gets worse at their job, but each segment gets a third of the attention, and each of those four factors degrades. Say each drops to 0.5 — still better than a coin flip on every dimension, which is generous. Multiply: about 6%.

Three segments at 6% each is 18% of a market's worth of effort, against 41% from one. You didn't lose a third. You lost most of it.

A caveat worth stating. These numbers are illustrative, not measured — I've used round figures to make the shape of the problem visible. The specific values will be different in your business. The multiplicative structure won't be: these factors compound rather than add, and that is the entire point.

Focus isn't a sacrifice you make to be disciplined. It's the only way the multiplication works in your favour.

Why it feels fine from the inside

If the cost is this steep, why doesn't it announce itself? Three reasons, all of them quiet.

The deals you win are the ones that didn't need you to be good

In every segment there are buyers who arrive pre-convinced — a warm introduction, an urgent deadline, a champion who already used your product elsewhere. Those close regardless of whether your message fits. So a scattered team still wins deals, and reads those wins as evidence the breadth is working. It isn't. It's evidence that some deals close themselves.

Losses get attributed one at a time

Nobody writes "we lost because we're spread too thin" in a CRM. They write "price", "timing", "went with incumbent". Each loss gets a local explanation, so the pattern never assembles into a single cause. The damage is systemic but the reporting is case-by-case.

Breadth is politically safe

Choosing one segment means telling a board, a sales team and possibly a co-founder that two of their bets are being paused. Keeping all three means nobody has to be told anything. The scattered strategy is rarely chosen; it's what's left when no choice is made. That's the real finding in most positioning work — not that the team picked wrong, but that the decision was never actually taken, and the campaign calendar made it by default.

Choosing the first segment, in an afternoon

The fear is that choosing requires a research project. It usually doesn't, because the answer is already in your data — you've just never sorted it this way. Pull your closed-won deals from the last eighteen months and score each candidate segment on four questions.

  1. Where do you already win fastest? Not where you have the most logos — where sales cycles are shortest. Speed is the market telling you the fit is real.
  2. Where do you lose least to "no decision"? Losing to a competitor means the category made sense and you didn't. Losing to no decision means the problem wasn't urgent. The second is far more expensive, and it's a segment signal.
  3. Where can you name the alternative precisely? If you can say exactly what this buyer does today without you — the tool, the spreadsheet, the person — you understand them. If the answer is vague, you don't, whatever the revenue says.
  4. Where would one more customer make the next one easier? Some wins compound because buyers in that segment talk to each other, attend the same events, read the same newsletter. Others are isolated. Compounding segments are worth more than their contract value suggests.

Score each segment one to five on all four. The winner is usually obvious within an hour, and it is frequently not the one with the biggest reported pipeline — because pipeline measures where you've been spending, not where you've been winning.

What "choosing" actually commits you to

This is where the conversation usually stalls, so let's be precise. Choosing a primary segment does not mean:

  • Refusing revenue from the other two. Take the deals. Cash is cash.
  • Removing them from the product roadmap.
  • Announcing a permanent decision. Revisit it in two quarters.

It means something narrower and more uncomfortable: your homepage, your proof and your outbound speak to one segment. The other two still buy — they just arrive through sales conversations and referrals rather than through marketing designed for them.

That's the whole trade. You are not giving up the revenue. You are giving up the comfort of a homepage that doesn't exclude anyone — and a homepage that excludes nobody is a homepage that convinces nobody. Every visitor who reads it is quietly asking "is this for me?" and a message built for three audiences answers "sort of" three times.

A message that tries not to lose anyone wins no one. "Sort of" is not a feeling that generates a demo request.

The test

If you want one question to take into your next leadership meeting, make it this one:

"Which customer would we be most upset to lose to a competitor, and does our homepage speak to them more clearly than to anyone else?"

When the two answers are the same, you're focused. When they aren't — and they usually aren't — you've found the gap, and it's a cheaper one to close than most people expect. It is a writing problem, not a strategy rebuild.
