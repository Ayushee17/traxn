Illustrative · not client workPositioning · Market entry

A composite scenario: an Indian contract-management SaaS, ₹20 crore revenue, preparing a UK launch.

The same product, positioned for Bengaluru and for London

At home the product wins against spreadsheets and email: the pitch is "stop losing contracts in inboxes". In the UK the buyer already has a tool, or a legal-ops lead who has evaluated three. The alternative changes, so everything downstream changes.

Bengaluru

Alternative
Email, shared drives, spreadsheets
Proof expected
Founder demo, a few local logos
Price anchor
Cost of one missed renewal
Headline that works
"Never lose a contract in an inbox again"

London

Alternative
An incumbent CLM tool, or a legal-ops evaluation already underway
Proof expected
UK or EU logos, security and data-residency answers, a G2 presence
Price anchor
The incumbent's per-seat fee
Headline that works
"CLM for the mid-market, at a third of the enterprise price, live in two weeks"
Insight
The home-market headline describes a problem UK buyers solved years ago. The differentiator that travels is speed and price against a known incumbent, not the problem itself.
Learning
Market entry is a positioning problem before it is a channel problem. Find the alternative in the new market first; the message follows.

Illustrative · not client workDemand generation · Measurement

A composite scenario: a 12-person marketing team running content, outbound and two events a quarter.

A measurement model for a small SaaS marketing team

The team reported traffic, followers and MQLs. Sales reported pipeline. Nobody could connect the two, so every budget review became an argument. The fix was a spreadsheet, not a data warehouse.

Insight
Of the deals created in a quarter, the first touch was recoverable for roughly three-quarters by asking one question on the first call and tagging the CRM. That was enough to see which programmes created pipeline and which created activity.
Strategy
Three programme-level metrics (qualified first calls, pipeline created, cost per qualified call), reviewed monthly, with one decision per review: scale, fix or stop.
Learning
Attribution doesn't need to be perfect to be useful. It needs to be consistent and honest about its gaps.

Illustrative · not client workPositioning · AI products

A composite scenario: a Series A AI company whose demo consistently impressed and whose deals consistently stalled.

The demo that wowed, and the deal that died in procurement

Every first call went well. Prospects called the product "magic". Then the second call never got booked, or it got booked with someone who asked questions the founder couldn't answer: where does the data go, what happens when it's wrong, who signs off on it. The company read this as a sales problem and hired two more AEs. It was a positioning problem.

What the company sold

Framing
A breakthrough in capability
Hero moment
The output, generated live
Champion
An enthusiast who loved the demo
Unanswered
Accuracy, liability, data residency, rollback

What the buyer was buying

Framing
A change to an existing workflow, with a new failure mode
Hero moment
What happens on the day it gets something wrong
Champion
Someone who must defend the decision internally
Required
An audit trail and a human checkpoint
Insight
The enthusiasm in call one and the silence after it came from two different people. The demo converted an individual; the purchase required a committee that had never seen the demo and was assessing risk, not capability.
Strategy
Stop selling the output and start selling the control surface around it: confidence scores, the review step, what the system refuses to do. Reposition against the honest alternative, which was not a competitor but "keep doing it manually and avoid the risk".
Learning
For AI products, the differentiator that closes deals is rarely the model. It is the evidence that someone has thought carefully about being wrong. Capability wins the first meeting; accountability wins the contract.

How we do this in a positioning engagement

Illustrative · not client workGo-to-market · GCC entry

A composite scenario: an Indian B2B SaaS company treating the UAE as "an easier US".

Why the Gulf is not a smaller version of your US plan

The reasoning sounds right: English-speaking, business-friendly, short flight, high willingness to pay. So the team reused the US playbook — inbound content, a self-serve trial, a pricing page in dollars — and waited. Traffic came. Pipeline did not.

Insight
The US plan assumed a buyer who researches privately, trials alone and arrives at sales pre-convinced. In the Gulf, the composite pattern ran the other way: introductions carried more weight than content, the first meeting was expected in person or by phone rather than as a form fill, and a local presence or partner was treated as a signal of permanence, not a nice-to-have.
Strategy
Invert the funnel for the market. Lead with partnerships and referenced introductions rather than inbound. Replace the self-serve trial with a guided pilot. Answer the questions that actually gate the deal — data residency, local entity, Arabic support, procurement cycles tied to the government calendar — on the page, before anyone has to ask.
Learning
Market entry fails most often where the market looks familiar. Genuine difference gets researched; apparent similarity gets assumed. The UAE punishes that assumption more quietly than a hard market would, because the traffic still arrives and only the pipeline is missing.

How we do this in a go-to-market engagement

Illustrative · not client workPositioning · Full engagement

A composite scenario: a 60-person HR tech company, ₹38 crore ARR, where the win rate looked healthy and pipeline was shrinking quarter on quarter.

The company that was winning deals and losing the market

This one is written in the full structure we use for published client cases, so you can see the shape of the work end to end. Every number below is modelled, not measured, and the assumptions are shown.

Context
A mid-market HR platform, nine years old, profitable, selling payroll and compliance to Indian companies of 200–2,000 staff. The board had asked for a path to doubling ARR in eight quarters.
Challenge
Win rate on qualified opportunities sat around 34%, which the team read as proof the product was strong. But the number of qualified opportunities had fallen for three consecutive quarters while marketing spend held flat. The internal explanation was "the market has slowed".
Insight
Interviews with recent losses and, more usefully, with buyers who never engaged at all, surfaced the real pattern: the company was winning the deals where it was already shortlisted, and almost never being shortlisted in the first place. The high win rate wasn't evidence of strength — it was evidence of a narrow top of funnel flattering the average. Prospects consistently described the category as "payroll software", while the company described itself as "a people operations platform", a phrase no buyer used in any of the conversations.
Strategy
Reposition into the category buyers actually searched for and asked peers about, and differentiate inside it rather than above it. Lead with compliance confidence — the thing losses said kept them awake — instead of breadth of modules, which every competitor also claimed. Rejected: a new category definition ("people ops"), which would have required educating the market the company could least afford to educate, and a price cut, which addressed a symptom nobody had raised.
Execution
Three weeks of research and definition, then a four-week build: homepage and two product pages rewritten against the new category, sales deck and discovery questions realigned, and a one-page internal positioning brief so product, sales and marketing described it identically. The first measurable change was the words, not the spend.
Results Modelled
If being described in the buyer's own category language lifts shortlist rate from 1 in 9 inbound conversations to 1 in 5 — the shift the interviews suggested was available — qualified opportunities roughly double at flat spend. At a steady 34% win rate that is the board's growth target without a single new channel. Assumptions: spend, pricing, sales headcount and win rate all held constant; only shortlist rate moves. A real engagement would measure this over two quarters before claiming it.
Learning
A high win rate is one of the most dangerous numbers in B2B marketing, because it looks like success and can be a symptom of invisibility. Always read it against the volume of opportunities that produced it. If you win most of the deals you're in and the pipeline is still shrinking, the problem is upstream of sales and no amount of sales improvement will reach it.

How we do this in a positioning engagement

Illustrative · not client workAI search · Visibility

A composite scenario: a well-regarded customer support SaaS that had stopped appearing in the answers its buyers were now asking for.

Invisible to the assistant, and nobody noticed for two quarters

Search traffic was stable, rankings were stable, and demo requests were quietly declining. The dashboards showed nothing wrong because they were not measuring the surface where buyers had moved.

Context
A support-desk product with strong G2 reviews and solid organic rankings for its main category terms, selling to support leads at companies of 100–1,000 staff in India and the UK.
Challenge
Sales began hearing "we had a shortlist already" more often, and the shortlists named two competitors the team considered weaker. Nothing in analytics explained it: the site's rankings hadn't moved.
Insight
Running twenty buyer-phrased prompts across three assistants, three times each, the company was named in a small minority of answers — and when it was named, it was described by a secondary feature rather than its actual strength. The competitors that dominated the answers didn't rank better in search. They were simply described more often in the kind of sources assistants lean on: comparison pages, review-site summaries, forum threads and analyst-style roundups, rather than their own marketing pages.
Strategy
Stop optimising the homepage for the assistant and start producing the material the assistant reads about you. Specifically: structured comparison content answering the exact questions buyers were putting to the assistant, factual and checkable claims in place of adjectives, and consistency of category language everywhere the company appeared off-site. Rejected: keyword-style "AI SEO" tactics, which assume the assistant works like a search engine and mostly waste a quarter.
Execution
A two-week visibility snapshot establishing the baseline with the full prompt set published, then a content plan scoped to one quarter, then re-measurement with the identical prompts on the same schedule.
Results Modelled
The honest version: visibility in AI answers is measurable and movable, but noisy. A reasonable target for one quarter is to go from named-in-a-minority to named-in-most answers for your core prompts, and — often more valuable — to be described correctly when named. Assumptions: same prompts, same assistants, same cadence, results read as a repeatable sample rather than a ranking. Anyone promising you a guaranteed position in an AI answer is selling something that cannot be bought.
Learning
You can hold every search ranking you've ever had and still disappear from the shortlist, because a growing share of research now ends in an answer rather than a results page. The first move isn't to produce more content — it's to measure what the assistants currently say about you, which almost no company has actually checked.

How we do this in an AI search engagement

Original research

What AI assistants say about B2B SaaS categories.

An ongoing study. We run a fixed set of buyer prompts across ChatGPT, Perplexity and Gemini and record which vendors are named, how they're described and which sources the answers draw on.

Method

Twenty prompts per category, written the way buyers ask (role, company size, market). Each run three times on different days. We log vendors named, position, description and cited or paraphrased sources.

Limits

Answers vary with wording, user context, location and time. Our results are a repeatable sample, not a ranking. We publish the prompts so anyone can re-run them.

Status

First categories in progress: contract management, HR tech and customer support software, for Indian and UK buyers. Findings will be published on the Insights page with the full prompt set.
